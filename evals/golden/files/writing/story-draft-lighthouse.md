# The Keeper of Varnholm Light (draft 2)

The lighthouse on Varnholm had not needed a keeper for eleven years, but nobody had told Edda Strand that, and so every evening at a quarter past six she climbed the hundred and four steps with her flask and her notebook and sat beside the lamp until it came on by itself.

She had been the last keeper. When the light was automated in 2015 the harbour board sent her a letter thanking her for thirty-one years of service, and a small brass clock, and a pension that was enough if she was careful. She had been careful all her life. It was not difficult to keep being careful.

The island had changed around her. The fish factory closed. The school went down to one classroom, then to none, and the children took the ferry to the mainland at seven every morning, their bags bumping against their knees as they ran down the jetty. The shop opened three days a week now. The post came on Tuesdays and Fridays. But the light still turned, and Edda still climbed the steps, and in her notebook she wrote down the same things she had always written: the wind, the sea, the ships she could see, and the time the lamp came on.

Nobody read the notebook. She knew this. There were forty-two of them now, on two shelves in her kitchen, and when she died someone from the mainland would put them in a box and the box would go somewhere damp. She had thought about this a great deal, mostly on the stairs, and had decided that it did not matter. The point of writing a thing down was not that someone would read it. The point was that you had looked.

In the autumn of the year this story is about, a boy started coming to the lighthouse.

His name was Tobias, and he was nine, and he lived with his grandfather in the white house by the old factory. His mother was working on the mainland, he said, and would come back at Christmas. Edda had heard otherwise from the woman at the shop, but she did not say so. He came on the first evening because he had followed her, curious about where an old woman went every night with a flask, and he came on the second evening because she had let him look through the binoculars, and after that he simply came.

He asked a great many questions. Why did the light turn? Why was it white and not red? What was the furthest ship she had ever seen? Had anyone ever been shipwrecked here? (Yes. Twice in her time. Everybody saved, both times. She told him about the second one, the trawler in the February storm, and left out the parts that still woke her up.) Why did she write things down if the light came on by itself?

She thought about that one before she answered.

"Because the light knows when it is dark," she said. "But it does not know what the dark was like."

Tobias considered this. Then he asked if he could write in the notebook too.

She let him. His handwriting was large and leaned backwards, and he spelled "horizon" three different ways in one week, and he wrote things she would never have written: that a gull had looked at him in a rude way, that the sea was the colour of his grandfather's jumper, that he was cold but did not want to go home yet. She did not correct any of it. On the evenings he did not come, she found she wrote less.

In December the weather turned. There was a week when the ferry did not run at all, and the boy's grandfather fell on the ice outside the shop and was taken to the mainland by the rescue boat, and Tobias stayed with Edda in her small house below the light. He slept on the sofa under three blankets. In the evenings they climbed the steps together, slowly, because her knees were bad that winter and he had learned to wait for her without being asked.

On the last evening before Christmas the ferry ran again. His mother was not on it. His grandfather was, with a stick and a bandage and a bag of oranges. Tobias went home. Edda climbed the steps alone and wrote the wind, and the sea, and the time the lamp came on.

Then she turned back a page and read what he had written the night before, in his leaning handwriting: "The light came on at 15:52. Edda says that is the earliest all year. It was very dark but not the bad kind."

She sat with that for a long time.

In January she wrote a letter to the harbour board, the first in eleven years. She asked whether, when the time came, the notebooks might be kept at the island library rather than sent to the mainland. And she asked whether a boy who had learned to read the weather might, in a few years, be shown how the light was serviced.

The reply took six weeks. It said yes to both.

She put it on the shelf beside the forty-third notebook, and at a quarter past six she climbed the stairs.
