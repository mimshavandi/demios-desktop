Segment: interview with a local beekeeper
Host: Clara Nyberg (CN)
Guest: Elias Brandvold (EB)
Automatic transcript, lightly corrected.

CN: Good morning, you're listening to Saturday Morning Neighbours. It's just gone half past ten, the sun's out, and I've got someone in the studio who, I'm told, has been stung more times than he can count. Elias Brandvold, welcome.

EB: Thank you, Clara. Yes, I stopped counting a long time ago. Somewhere in the first year, I think.

CN: How long have you been keeping bees?

EB: Thirty years this spring. I started with one hive that I bought from an old man in Brückstadt who was giving up. He gave me the hive, a veil with a hole in it, and about twenty minutes of advice. These days I've got twenty-two hives, spread over four sites around Haldorf: two orchards, a farm on the Lindenfeld road, and the allotments behind the station.

CN: Twenty-two! So is this a business now, or still a hobby?

EB: Somewhere in between. It pays for itself and a bit more, but I still have my day job, I'm a surveyor. My wife says it's a hobby that takes over every weekend from April to September, and she's right.

CN: Let's talk about this year. How has 2026 been for the bees?

EB: Mixed. The winter was hard. I went into winter with twenty-two colonies and lost six of them by March. That's about twenty-seven percent, which is high. A normal winter for me is maybe ten, fifteen percent losses.

CN: What happened?

EB: A few things, I think. We had that very mild spell in January, the bees flew, used up energy, there was nothing to forage, and then the cold came back. And I think, honestly, I was a bit late with my mite treatment in the autumn, so some colonies went into winter weaker than they should have. That's on me.

CN: Mites. Tell us about that, because I think a lot of listeners will have heard of the varroa mite without really knowing what it is.

EB: Right. Varroa is a tiny mite, about the size of a pinhead, reddish brown. It lives on the bees and, more importantly, it breeds inside the brood cells, where the young bees are developing. It weakens them and it spreads viruses. Almost every colony in this part of the world has some varroa. The question isn't whether you have it, it's how many.

CN: And what do you do about it?

EB: You monitor it. I count the mites that fall onto a sheet under the hive, or I do a sugar roll, where you shake a small sample of bees in powdered sugar and count the mites that come off. If the numbers are high, you treat. Most beekeepers round here use treatments based on organic acids, oxalic acid in winter when there's no brood, formic acid in late summer. The timing matters enormously. That was my mistake last year: I treated in late September instead of August, and by then the damage to the winter bees was done.

CN: So what's the lesson for this year?

EB: Treat in August, straight after taking the honey off. I did that this year, and the counts look much better.

CN: Let's get to the good news then. Honey.

EB: Ha, yes. Despite starting the season with fewer colonies, it's been a good honey year. The spring was warm and the lime trees flowered really well in late June. I took off about four hundred and ten kilos this year, which is one of my better years.

CN: Four hundred and ten kilos! What happens to all of that?

EB: Most of it goes in jars. I sell five-hundred-gram jars for nine euros, at the Saturday market in Haldorf and at two farm shops. Some goes to a bakery in town that makes honey cake. And a fair bit gets given away to the landowners where the hives are, that's the deal: they get honey, I get somewhere to put my bees.

CN: People listening will want to know: is there a difference between your honey and supermarket honey?

EB: I'll try not to be a snob about it. Local honey tastes of where it comes from. My orchard honey in spring is pale and mild, the summer honey from the lime trees is stronger, a bit minty almost. Supermarket honey is often a blend from many places, which is fine, it's consistent, but it doesn't change through the year the way mine does.

CN: Let's talk about people who want to start. I get emails every spring from listeners saying, "I want to save the bees, should I get a hive?" What do you tell them?

EB: [laughs] I tell them to slow down a bit. First of all, honeybees aren't really the ones that need saving. They're managed, like livestock. It's the wild bees, the solitary bees and bumblebees, that are in more trouble. If you want to help bees, the single best thing most people can do is plant the right flowers and leave a messy corner in the garden.

CN: But if they really do want honeybees?

EB: Then do it properly. Number one: do a course first. The Haldorf beekeepers' association runs a beginners' course every February, six evenings and two practical Saturdays. Number two: join the association, because you'll get a mentor, someone you can phone when you open the hive and something looks wrong. Number three: start with two hives, not one.

CN: Why two?

EB: Because then you can compare them. If one colony looks odd, you've got the other one to compare against. And if one fails, you can use the other to help it, move a frame of eggs across so they can raise a new queen. With one hive, you've got nothing to compare and no back-up.

CN: What about the cost?

EB: Honestly, budget around six hundred to eight hundred euros for the first year for two hives, the bees themselves, a suit, gloves, a smoker and the basic tools. You can buy second-hand, but be careful, old equipment can carry diseases. Get it checked or scorch it.

CN: And time?

EB: In the season, half an hour to an hour per hive every week or so. In May and June, during swarm season, more. And you have to be around: you can't go off for three weeks in May and expect to come back to the same colony.

CN: Swarming. Tell us about swarms, because every year we get calls in the studio about a "huge ball of bees" in someone's hedge.

EB: Yes, I get those calls too! Swarming is how a colony reproduces. The old queen leaves with about half the bees and they hang in a cluster, often on a branch, while scouts look for a new home. It looks dramatic but swarming bees are usually very gentle, because they've filled up on honey before leaving and they've got no home to defend.

CN: So what should someone do if they find one?

EB: Don't spray it, don't try to move it yourself. Phone the association's swarm line, there's a list of collectors on their website, and someone will come out, usually the same day. We're glad to collect them, it's free bees for us.

CN: And as a beekeeper, how do you stop your own bees swarming?

EB: You inspect every seven to nine days in swarm season and look for queen cells, they look like little peanuts hanging off the bottom of the frames. If you find them, you split the colony, you make an artificial swarm, you give them space. It's a bit of an art, and every beekeeper has a story about a swarm that got away. I lost two this May, actually. Both from the station allotments, both on the same weekend, while I was at my niece's wedding.

CN: [laughs] So you weren't around in May, which you just told us not to do.

EB: Exactly. Do as I say, not as I do.

CN: What should people plant if they want to help bees, honeybees or wild ones?

EB: Think about the whole year, not just summer. In early spring, things like crocus, willow and fruit blossom. In summer, lavender, thyme, marjoram, and phacelia if you've got a bit of bare ground, it's a cover crop with blue flowers that bees love. And late in the year, ivy. People cut ivy back but its flowers in September and October are one of the last big food sources before winter. If you've got ivy flowering on a wall, leave it.

CN: Anything to avoid?

EB: Double flowers, the very frilly varieties, often have no pollen or nectar the bees can reach. They look nice but they're like a closed shop. And go easy on garden chemicals, obviously.

CN: Before we finish, I heard you're starting something with a school.

EB: Yes, I'm really excited about this. From spring next year, Ashgrove Primary is going to have an observation hive, that's a hive with glass sides, inside the building, with a tube out through the wall so the bees come and go. The children can watch them without any risk. And we'll plant a bee border along the playground fence. We're looking for volunteers, especially people who are good at gardening, to help with the border and with a few sessions with the children.

CN: How do people get involved?

EB: They can email me at elias@haldorf-bees.test, or come and find me at my stall at the Saturday market, I'm there most weeks until the end of October.

CN: Elias Brandvold, thank you so much for coming in.

EB: Thank you, Clara. And go and plant some ivy.

CN: [laughs] You heard him. After the news, we'll be talking to the organisers of the Haldorf autumn food festival. Stay with us.
